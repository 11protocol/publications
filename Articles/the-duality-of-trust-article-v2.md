# The Duality of Trust in High-Stakes Human/AI Decisions

On December 28, 1978, a United Airlines DC-8 circled over Portland for nearly an hour while its captain — a veteran with a flawless record — worked a minor landing-gear problem. The flight engineer flagged the fuel state more than once. The warnings were acknowledged, not acted on. The aircraft ran out of fuel and crashed six miles short of the runway [[1]](https://www.thisdayinaviation.com/tag/united-airlines-flight-173/). The NTSB's finding wasn't that the captain lacked skill. It was that his attention, and the crew's deference to it, never adjusted to a situation his experience hadn't prepared him for [[1]](https://www.thisdayinaviation.com/tag/united-airlines-flight-173/).

We build trust on track record. A model's accuracy. A person's tenure. Both function the same way: they tell you how something has behaved across everything it's already faced. What neither one tells you is how it behaves on the one situation that doesn't look like the rest — and in business, that's usually the situation with the most at stake.

## A Clean Record Only Covers What's Already Been Tested

Trust built on track record works exactly as long as the future resembles the past. Behavioral research on expert judgment — most notably a 2009 review by Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein, researchers on opposite sides of a long-running debate about when intuition can be trusted — found that expert intuition is reliable only where the environment is regular enough to learn from and the expert got fast, clear feedback while learning it [[2]](https://www.managementcraft.co/sources/kahneman-klein-conditions-intuitive-expertise). Outside that — genuinely novel situations, one-off high-stakes calls — experience produces confidence, not accuracy [[2]](https://www.managementcraft.co/sources/kahneman-klein-conditions-intuitive-expertise). The feeling of certainty doesn't come from being right. It comes from how coherent the story sounds, and a fluent story and a correct one aren't the same thing.

The argument this piece makes — and one a deeper technical review of the same research walks through at greater length [[3]](https://github.com/11protocol/publications/blob/main/Articles/The%20Duality%20of%20Trust%20in%20High-Stakes%20Human-AI%20Decisions.md) — is that this isn't a limitation unique to human judgment or to any one type of machine. It's a structural property of any defined system, whether that system runs on human judgment, fixed rules, or statistical inference. What differs is *how* each one tends to break — and CXOs evaluating risk need to know which kind of break they're actually exposed to.

## Two Different Machines, Two Different Ways to Break

"AI risk" gets treated as one category, but the machines making decisions in most businesses split into two very different kinds, and they fail for opposite reasons.

Deterministic systems — the rule-based code that runs payroll, trading logic, or accounting reconciliation — have no concept of uncertainty at all. They execute exactly what's written, every time, with no capacity to hesitate when a case falls outside what the rules anticipated. In 2012, Knight Capital Group deployed updated trading software to one of eight servers incorrectly, leaving a dormant, years-old piece of code active on the ninth. When the market opened, that code executed flawlessly according to its own logic — logic nobody had intended to still be running. In 45 minutes it generated millions of unintended trades, cost the firm more than $460 million, and forced its sale within the year [[4]](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf). Nothing in that system was "wrong" in a statistical sense — it had no statistics. It simply had no way to represent the fact that the situation it was in was not the situation it was built for.

Probabilistic systems — the statistical and machine-learning models most people mean when they say "AI" — tend to be the mirror image. They're built specifically to represent uncertainty, expressed as a confidence or accuracy score. That sounds like an advantage, until the model is asked to operate outside the data that produced that score. Researchers have documented that modern models tend toward exactly this failure: high accuracy paired with poor calibration, meaning a system can be confidently wrong in a way its own numbers never flag [[5]](https://davidstutz.de/on-calibration-of-modern-neural-networks-guo-et-al/). In general terms, a rules-based system is more likely to fail because it has no way to register that a new situation exists at all, while a statistical system is more likely to fail because it does register one, but keeps reporting a confidence score calibrated to a world that no longer applies.

## When the Warning Signs Are There and Still Don't Register

The Epic Sepsis Model, a proprietary early-warning tool embedded in electronic health records at hundreds of U.S. hospitals, shows what that second failure mode looks like in practice. Before wide deployment, the vendor's own figures put its accuracy in a range that read as clinically useful [[6]](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/articlepdf/2781313/jamainternal_habib_2021_ed_210007_1627675039.49003.pdf?resultClick=1). Hospitals built workflows around that number.

In 2021, researchers at the University of Michigan tested the model against real hospital data — nearly 38,500 hospitalizations — and found its real-world accuracy fell far short of what had been claimed, missing roughly two-thirds of actual sepsis cases while flagging so many false alarms that clinicians had to chase over a hundred alerts to find one real one [[7]](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/popular-sepsis-prediction-tool-less-accurate-claimed). The model itself hadn't gotten worse — its published accuracy simply reflected the population and conditions it was validated on, not necessarily those of the hospitals now depending on it, and that number gave no visible signal of the gap until an outside party went and checked [[7]](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/popular-sepsis-prediction-tool-less-accurate-claimed).

## What Happens When Nobody Is Positioned to Catch the Gap

The costliest version of this problem isn't a deterministic system failing, or a probabilistic one, or a person failing. It's an organization built so that no one is positioned to catch any of their blind spots — because everyone is deferring to the same unexamined track record.

Between 1999 and 2015, the UK Post Office prosecuted hundreds of subpostmasters for theft and false accounting based on shortfalls reported by its Horizon accounting system — a deterministic, rules-based platform, not a learning model. More than 700 convictions ultimately traced back to Horizon evidence [[8]](https://www.computerweekly.com/feature/Post-Office-Horizon-scandal-explained-everything-you-need-to-know). The shortfalls were software bugs. When branch operators reported that the numbers didn't match reality, investigators worked from a legal and institutional assumption that computer systems are reliable by default — an assumption UK law had explicitly built in years earlier [[9]](https://journals.sas.ac.uk/deeslr/article/download/5642/5310/9815) — so a person's word was weighed against a machine's track record, and the machine won by default. People went to prison. Some took their own lives. It stands as one of the widest miscarriages of justice in UK history [[8]](https://www.computerweekly.com/feature/Post-Office-Horizon-scandal-explained-everything-you-need-to-know).

The public inquiry into the scandal points to a mix of causes rather than one — legal presumption, institutional culture, and years of dismissing subpostmasters' own accounts. One reading that sits underneath several of those causes: no single role in that chain had both the position and the mandate to test what the system's track record actually proved versus what it was being assumed to prove. That's an argument about the case, not a finding the inquiry itself frames in exactly these terms.

## What Actually Earns Trust When the Record Runs Out

None of this argues for defaulting to the human, the rules engine, or the model. All three cases above show the same failure wearing different clothes: a track record was mistaken for a guarantee, exactly where a guarantee was needed most.

What holds up in fields that have already been burned by this isn't a better track record — it's structure that doesn't depend on one. Banking regulators, after the 2008 financial crisis, stopped accepting a model's historical accuracy as sufficient grounds for trust; current model-risk guidance requires institutions to independently test *why* a model works, not just confirm that it has, and to keep re-testing as conditions change rather than trusting a one-time validation [[10]](https://ryanoconnellfinance.com/model-risk-management/). Aviation, after Portland and crashes like it, rebuilt cockpit procedure so a junior crew member's contrary observation carries structural weight regardless of the captain's seniority — because the whole point is to catch the case where the senior person's experience doesn't apply [[11]](https://rustymedic.com/crew-resource-management-101-why-great-crews-beat-great-medics/).

The common thread isn't oversight for its own sake. It's making the reasoning behind a decision visible *before* the outcome is known, and keeping a live record of what was assumed, what was flagged, and what happened next — so the gap between "this has worked before" and "this will work here" gets caught by the system, not discovered afterward by whoever happens to be paying attention.

## Where This Puts the Burden on Your Business

Every one of these failures was survivable, right up until it wasn't — and every one of them had a moment where the organization could have asked a simple question and didn't: does this specific track record actually cover this specific decision?

In many organizations, that question doesn't have a clear owner. Deterministic systems tend to get signed off by IT. Probabilistic models tend to get signed off by whoever bought or built them. Human judgment calls tend to get signed off by tenure and title. Read back across the three cases above with that in mind: each failure took hold inside a lane that its own owner had every reason to trust — engineering's deployment process, a vendor's stated accuracy, a legal presumption of reliability — and nobody sitting outside that lane was positioned to test the assumption before it broke. None of the underlying investigations frame it in exactly these terms; that's this piece's reading of what the three cases have in common, not a conclusion any of them reached on their own.

If you're a CXO, the useful exercise isn't asking whether your organization trusts AI too much or too little. It's mapping your highest-stakes decisions against which of the three categories actually makes them — a person relying on experience, a deterministic system executing fixed rules, or a probabilistic model producing a confidence score — and asking, for each one, what evidence you actually have that the track record behind it covers the situation it's being trusted with. Where that evidence doesn't exist, that's not a technology gap. It's a governance gap, and it's the one most likely to surface at the worst possible time.
